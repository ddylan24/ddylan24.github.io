# S10: Jump risk in near-certain contracts

**Question.** S9 found 90–98¢ contracts move about twice as much as the diffusion law predicts, and S3 found buying them loses money. How fat is the tail?

## Method (`code/s10_jumps.py`)
- Polymarket daily paths (P2), with each side counted as its own contract when it sits at ≥90¢. That's 44,884 contract-days in 364 events.
- Diffusion benchmark: daily sd = c·φ(N⁻¹p)/τ^0.53 (S9 law), with c = 0.938 fitted on 20–80% states, assuming normal changes.

## Results
| one-day drop from ≥90¢ | actual | normal diffusion | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| ≥10¢ | 0.31% (141) | 0.056% | ×6 |
| ≥20¢ | 0.12% (54) | 0.003% | ×39 |
| ≥40¢ | 0.033% (15) | ≈0 | ×2,800 |

- Standardized changes have **kurtosis ≈ 1,000**. |z| > 4 happens 2.0% of the time, against 0.006% under a normal.
- Eventual loss rate per contract-day:
  - 90–95¢: **9.1% lost vs 7.2% implied by price**. Overpriced, consistent with S3.
  - 95–98¢: 3.6% vs 3.3%. Close to fair.
  - (Contract-days overlap within a market, so these are descriptive.)
- **Model implication.** Near-certain prices follow a *jump* process: long calm stretches, then a news shock (an upset, a reversal, a rule clarification). A jump-diffusion (Merton-style, as in the earlier jump module) or a two-state hazard model fits better than pure diffusion. Expected loss lives almost entirely in the jumps.

## Lessons
1. Never size or risk-manage a "safe" binary with a diffusion or normal VaR. The 20¢ tail is 39× more likely than that predicts.
2. Selling insurance (buying 90–95¢ favorites) is short jump risk and was overpriced here. Market makers quoting near 95¢ need wider spreads or smaller size than diffusion vol suggests.
3. Measure tails directly, by counting exceedances, rather than inferring them from variance.
