# R5: Shin's model and removing the "vig" from multi-outcome prices

**Problem.** The quoted prices of a complete set of outcomes sum to more than 1. That excess is the overround or "vig". Kalshi weather mids sum to a median 1.045 per city-day, and asks to 1.090. To read probabilities you have to remove it. How you remove it matters, because the excess is *not* spread evenly.

## Methods (π_i = quoted prices, Σπ = 1 + overround)
- **Multiplicative:** p_i = π_i / Σπ. This assumes the excess is proportional to probability.
- **Additive:** p_i = π_i − (Σπ − 1)/n. Equal cents off each outcome, so longshots lose relatively more.
- **Power:** p_i = π_i^k with k chosen so Σ = 1 (k > 1 shades longshots).
- **Shin (1991, 1993).** The bookmaker (here, the market maker) knows a fraction z of order flow is from insiders who know the winner. It widens prices so that losses to insiders are covered by uninformed flow. Inverting the bookmaker's pricing problem gives
  `p_i = [√(z² + 4(1−z) π_i²/Σπ) − z] / (2(1−z))`, with z solved so Σp = 1.
  - Longshots carry relatively more insider risk, so Shin shades them down more. This is a *microstructure* explanation of the favorite-longshot bias, an alternative to the risk-preference story in R3.
  - Illustration: quotes summing to 1.12 with a 2% longshot: multiplicative 1.92%, **Shin 0.94%**, power 1.37%.

## Test on real outcomes (`code/r5_shin.py`)
199 complete Kalshi weather city-day events (6 buckets each, exactly one wins; data from N2, Aug–Oct 2026):

| method | Brier/event (mid) | log loss (mid) | Σ prob on <10¢ buckets (actual wins 14) |
|---|---|---|---|
| multiplicative | 0.6277 | 1.193 | 18.9 |
| power | 0.6261 | 1.187 | 17.7 |
| Shin (z ≈ 0.9%) | 0.6265 | 1.186 | 16.9 |
| additive | **0.6257** | **1.179** | 15.5 |

- Shin beats multiplicative on log loss by 0.007 per event (t −3.4 on mids, −4.3 on asks). The overround sits disproportionately in the longshots, the same favorite-longshot pattern found in N2 (logit slope 1.18).
- On this sample the even simpler **additive** method is best. Plain proportional normalization is the worst choice.
- Implied insider share: z ≈ 0.9% on mids and ≈ 1.9% on asks. Sports-betting studies (e.g., Shin 1993 on UK racing) report z of a few percent.
- Only 199 events from two months, so treat the ranking among shading methods as tentative. The "don't use multiplicative" conclusion is significant.

## Lessons
1. Never read probabilities off a vigged price set by simple division when longshots are involved. Shade longshots more (additive, power or Shin).
2. Shin's z is a usable "informed-flow" gauge: compare it across markets and over time.
3. This is the same favorite-longshot fact seen three ways: calibration slope (N2), the de-vig method (R5) and equilibrium theory (R3). Note the Polymarket evidence ran the other way (reverse bias, P2), so apply this per venue and category.
