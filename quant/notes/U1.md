# U1: Recession contracts are insurance. Risk premium in macro prediction markets

**Question.** T4 found election contracts have no market beta, so no risk premium. A recession contract pays exactly when wealth falls. Does it carry an insurance premium, trading above the true probability?

## Math
- Let dP/dM be the change in the contract price per unit market return (dollars per 100% move).
- CAPM: E[return per $ invested] = β·ERP with β = (dP/dM)/P.
- Over horizon T, the expected payoff q relates to price P by q ≈ P·(1 + β·ERP·T). So **P − q ≈ −(dP/dM)·ERP·T**. For a contract with negative beta, the price exceeds the probability.
- **Scaling note:** b ¢ per 1% move = 100·b ¢ per 100% move. I slipped on this in the first pass of this topic and in T4: T4's premium was understated (0.00¢ → 0.1¢, conclusion unchanged).

## Data (`code/u1_recession.py`)
- Polymarket daily prices for "US recession in 2025?" ($11.7M volume, resolved **NO**) and "US recession by end of 2026?" ($2.3M, now 7.5¢).
- SPY daily returns. Alignment as in T4: Polymarket's 00:00 UTC stamp is the previous evening ET.

## Results
| market | days | mean price | ¢ per 1% SPY | t | corr | corr on \|SPY\|>2% days | CAPM β per $ | premium |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recession in 2025 | 245 | 0.214 | **−0.96** | −4.9 | −0.49 | **−0.83** (n 14) | −4.5 | **≈ +5.5¢** (25% of price) |
| Recession by end-2026 | 247 | 0.222 | −0.42 | −2.3 | −0.19 | −0.94 (n 4) | −1.9 | ≈ +2.8¢ (13%) |

- **Recession contracts are strongly negative-beta**, especially on big market days. The April 2025 tariff shock drove both together.
- Under CAPM with a 5% equity premium, fair pricing puts the contract **3–5¢ above the true probability**. Buyers pay for insurance; sellers earn a premium for holding recession risk.
- The 2025 contract averaged 21¢ and resolved NO. That's consistent with a premium but proves nothing with n = 1.
- **Contrast with elections (T4: ≈0.1¢)**: the risk premium is outcome-specific. Read macro-disaster contracts as *risk-neutral* probabilities, which are biased upward. Read elections and sports as roughly *physical* probabilities.

## Lessons
1. Before reading a price as a probability, ask whether the outcome is correlated with aggregate wealth. If it is, expect a premium of several cents.
2. Selling recession, crash or disaster contracts is selling insurance: positive expected return, but losses arrive exactly when everything else loses. Size it like a short put, not a coin flip.
