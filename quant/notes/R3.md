# R3: Prices as beliefs. When does a prediction-market price equal the mean belief?

**Papers.** Manski (2006, *Econ Letters*), "Interpreting the predictions of prediction markets". Wolfers & Zitzewitz (2006, NBER w12200), "Interpreting prediction market prices as probabilities". Gjerstad (2005), "Risk aversion, beliefs, and prediction market equilibrium".

## Model
- Traders i have belief q_i, wealth w_i and CRRA utility U(W) = W^{1−γ}/(1−γ) (log for γ = 1).
- A binary contract pays $1, costs p, and is in zero net supply. Buying x YES gives W_yes = w + x(1−p) and W_no = w − xp.
- First-order condition: q(1−p)U'(W_yes) = (1−q)p U'(W_no). This gives
  `x = w (k − 1)/(1 − p + k p)`, with `k = [q(1−p) / ((1−q)p)]^{1/γ}`.
- **Log utility (γ = 1):** x = w(q − p)/(p(1−p)). Market clearing Σx = 0 gives **p = Σ w_i q_i / Σ w_i**, the wealth-weighted mean belief, *exactly*.
- **Risk neutral (Manski):** everyone goes all-in. Clearing requires the share of traders with q > π to equal π, so the price is the (1−π) quantile of beliefs. The mean belief is then only bounded: **(π², 2π − π²)**. A price of 0.10 is consistent with a mean belief anywhere from 0.01 to 0.19.

## Simulation (`code/r3_beliefs.py`, 20,000 traders, Beta beliefs, equal wealth)
Price − mean belief, in cents:

| mean belief | belief sd | γ=0.1 | γ=0.5 | γ=1 | γ=2 | γ=5 | risk-neutral |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.02 | +1.8 | +0.4 | 0 | −0.2 | −0.3 | +3.0 |
| 0.05 | 0.10 | +6.8 | +3.1 | 0 | −2.6 | −4.0 | +7.8 |
| 0.20 | 0.10 | +4.4 | +1.4 | 0 | −0.9 | −1.6 | +5.8 |
| 0.50 | 0.10 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 0.80 | 0.10 | −4.5 | −1.4 | 0 | +0.9 | +1.6 | −5.8 |

- **Log utility gives price = mean.** Otherwise the deviation grows with **belief dispersion** and is zero at 0.5.
- **Low risk aversion (γ < 1) produces the classic favorite-longshot bias:** longshots overpriced, favorites underpriced. Optimists in a longshot bet large because they're not very risk averse.
- **High risk aversion (γ > 1) produces the *reverse* bias:** longshots underpriced.
- So the *sign* of a calibration bias tells you something about who is trading, assuming the crowd's mean belief is right. In the data: Kalshi weather showed the classic bias (logit slope 1.18), which looks like aggressive/retail behavior; Polymarket 2025–26 showed the reverse (YES underpriced, slope ≈ 0.8). Other explanations remain (time value, tick floors, fees, prospect-theory probability weighting in Snowberg–Wolfers), so this is a consistent story, not proof.
- **Wealth matters.** With wealth correlated 0.43 with belief, the simple mean belief was 0.284 while the price (= wealth-weighted mean) was **0.479**. Prices aggregate *money-weighted* beliefs. Whales move the "probability."
- **Concentrated optimists.** With 10% of traders at 0.90 and 90% at 0.02, the mean is 0.108, the Manski (risk-neutral) price is 0.100, and the log-utility price is 0.108.

## Lessons
1. A price is a probability only under assumptions: log-ish utility, wealth independent of beliefs, no budget corners. Wolfers–Zitzewitz show the deviation is small for realistic dispersion, and the table above confirms ≤ 1–2¢ unless beliefs are very dispersed.
2. Deviations are largest for **longshots with dispersed beliefs**. That's where both theory and the data (P2, N1) put the mispricing.
3. Treat a price as the **money-weighted** crowd belief, which a few large accounts can move. That's relevant to R2 (skill vs luck) and to manipulation worries.
