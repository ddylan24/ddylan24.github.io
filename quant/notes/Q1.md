# Q1 — Logical (combinatorial) consistency across linked prediction markets

**Question.** Linked markets must obey probability logic. Do live Polymarket prices violate it, and can the violations be traded?

## The constraints
For the 2028 US presidential markets on Polymarket:
1. **Subset rule.** "X wins the presidency" ⊂ "X wins their party's nomination" (ignoring an independent run), so P(win) ≤ P(nominee). This is executable when **winner bid > nominee ask**: sell winner YES, buy nominee YES. You can never lose, because a payout on the leg you sold implies a payout on the leg you bought.
2. **Additivity.** The candidates of a party are disjoint, so Σ_{c∈party} P(c wins) ≤ P(party wins), with equality only if every possible winner is listed.
3. **Completeness.** Σ over all outcomes in the winner market should be 1. If it's below 1, the list is missing mass (there's no "Other").

## Data and method
Live gamma `/events` best bids/asks on 2026-10-14. The four events are 'Presidential Election Winner 2028' (54 candidates), 'Democratic / Republican Presidential Nominee 2028' (53 / 43) and 'Which party wins 2028 US Presidential Election?'. Candidates are matched across markets by a normalized-name key. Script: `code/q1_logic.py`.

## Results
- **Subset rule.** 27 Democrats and 25 Republicans matched. The one executable violation is **Dwayne "The Rock" Johnson**: winner bid exceeds Dem-nominee ask by **0.4¢**, and the mids imply P(win | nominated) = 1.50, which is impossible. Every other candidate sits at the 0.1¢ tick floor (ratio 1.00), and the largest non-executable ratio is Massie at 1.12. The book is tiny, it's a longshot, and the money is locked up until Nov 2028.
- **Completeness.** Σ winner-market mids = **0.919** (Σ asks 0.947). The winner list is missing about 8% of probability mass, so buying every YES at 0.947 is *not* an arb: an unlisted winner pays nothing, and T-bills alone return 1/e^{−0.0425·2.07} ≈ 1.09.
- **Additivity.**
  - Democrats: Σ candidate mids **0.530** vs party **0.635**. That's consistent with the missing mass, since an unlisted Democrat could win. Not a violation.
  - Republicans: Σ candidate mids **0.384** vs party **0.345**, so the candidates are *over*-priced relative to the party. Executable form: Σ winner **bids** over the 24 Republican names (excluding the one overlapping name) = **0.371** vs party **ask 0.350**, a **+2.1¢** gap. The trade is: sell 24 candidate YES (buy their NOs), buy Republican-party YES.
  - Caveats: (a) not riskless, because a Republican-listed name (e.g., Massie) could win as an independent. (b) Many legs are at the 0.1¢ floor with tiny size. (c) Capital is locked for 2 years: buying 24 NOs costs about $23.6 per $1 of gap. (d) Fees.

## Interpretation
- The deep single-market books are consistent with logic to about a cent.
- The inconsistencies sit in (i) longshot tails and (ii) *cross-event* sums that no single order book enforces.
- This matches **Saguillo et al. (2025)**: about $40M of realized combinatorial/rebalancing arbitrage on Polymarket, dominated by a few bots. Residual gaps are fee-sized, or locked up for so long that the annualized return is below T-bills.
- Rule of thumb: an arbitrage gap g with lockup T only beats cash if g / (capital per unit) > e^{rT} − 1. With capital ≈ $23.6 per unit here, a 2.1¢ gap returns 0.09% over two years. **Worthless.**

## Lessons
- Logical arbitrage needs (a) an exact entailment ("X wins ⇒ X nominated" is *not* exact, because of independents), (b) executable prices, not mids, (c) enough size, and (d) short lockup. Check all four.
- A market that sums to less than 1 is often *incomplete*, not underpriced.
- Neg-risk conversion (P34) fixes consistency *within* one event. Nothing enforces it *across* events. That's where bots hunt.
