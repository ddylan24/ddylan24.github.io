Why Thomas says retail does it backwards

QP 01 He describes the standard retail path as four steps, each of which serves the seller:

3. Work on psychology and disciplinePuts the blame on you when it fails. "If every strategy works with perfect discipline, why do funds pay PhDs millions to design strategies that machines execute with no psychology at all?"

He backs this up: he coded strategies from popular influencers (TJR, Justin Werlein, ICT concepts), and he says all of them did worse than claimed, many of them terribly. His replacement is the institutional workflow: start from an idea, test whether it has predictive power, and only then build a strategy around it.

One place I'd push back. "There should be no prolonged and inexplicable losing" is too strong. Even a real edge has long losing stretches. A strategy with a true Sharpe ratio of 0.5, which is a good long-run strategy, still loses money in about 31% of years and over 19% of 3-year periods. At Sharpe 1.0 it's 16% of years. Validation tells you what losing stretch is normal for your strategy, so you can tell bad luck from a broken edge (Module 09).

Module 03

Where edge comes from

QP 02 Thomas's definition: outside of arbitrage, trading means finding conditions under which the distribution of future outcomes differs from normal. A real difference needs a reason to exist. Ask which of these three your idea is:

  1. Risk premium. You get paid for holding a risk others don't want, such as crash risk, liquidity risk or value stocks in recessions. These edges persist but come with painful drawdowns.
  2. Behavioral. People under-react to news and then over-react. Momentum is the classic example.
  3. Structural. Someone has to trade for reasons unrelated to price: hedgers, index rebalancing, forced sellers. You're paid for supplying liquidity.

QP 03 He adds where an individual can compete: better data (harder to get, so fewer people use it) or better interpretation (a smarter model of common data). High-frequency firms also compete on faster execution, but for retail traders it's almost always one of those two.

Carry: collecting interest-rate differences

carry return = (S_t+1 / S_t)·(1 + i_foreign/12) − (1 + i_USD/12) uncovered interest parity (UIP): high-rate currencies should depreciate by the rate gap → carry earns 0

Using FRED daily exchange rates and OECD 3-month rates for 9 major currencies against the dollar (2002–2025), I went long the 3 highest-rate currencies and short the 3 lowest each month, using last month's rates:

  • 2.5% a year, Sharpe 0.35, skew −0.63. +45% in 2002–2007 (Sharpe 1.08), then −26.5% in 2008 (worst month −10.6%, September 2008), then +60% since 2009.
  • UIP fails. The rate gap paid +3.1% a year, and high-rate currencies fell only 0.6% a year, not the 3.1% UIP predicts. In Fama's regression the slope is −0.13 (t −0.35): closer to "exchange rates are a random walk" (0) than to UIP (−1). This is the forward-premium puzzle.
  • Carry is short crash risk. Its monthly return correlates −0.46 with the change in VIX. "Up the stairs, down the elevator": steady gains, sudden losses when fear spikes. It's a risk premium in the same family as selling volatility.

Low risk, high reward: the flat security market line

The most volatile stocks were the worst investment of all. The leading explanation (Frazzini & Pedersen 2014) is that investors who can't or won't borrow get extra risk by buying high-beta stocks, which bids them up. Lottery-seeking does the same to the most volatile names. It's a structural edge, and capturing it requires leverage.

Momentum's hidden risk: crashes

Stock momentum (Ken French's UMD factor, 1927–2026) earned 7.4% a year with a Sharpe of 0.45. But its skew is −3.0 and its kurtosis 30, and its worst months are brutal:

Every crash happened while the market rallied. The mechanism (Daniel & Moskowitz 2016): after a bear market, momentum is short the beaten-down losers, which are high-beta stocks. Its market beta flips from +0.05 in normal times to −0.64 after a bear market. When the market rebounds, the short side explodes. After a 24-month market decline, momentum averaged −2.6% a month in up-market months.

The fix is volatility scaling (Barroso & Santa-Clara 2015). Size momentum by 12% ÷ its trailing 6-month volatility. On the same data, that raised the Sharpe from 0.45 to 0.87, cut the skew from −3.0 to −0.4, and shrank the worst month from −53% to −29%. It works far better here than on the market itself (Module 10), because momentum's crash risk is visible in its own volatility before it hits.

"Public strategies can't have edge" needs one refinement. Thomas (QP 01) argues that once an edge is made public it ceases to exist. That holds for mispricings: they get arbitraged away, and published signals lose about 58% of their returns (Module 09). Risk premia are different. Everything in the table above is public, and much of it still pays, because you're being paid to bear a risk most people don't want. It shrinks, but it doesn't vanish. Your own private, event-based edges will be mispricings, so his rule applies to them: keep them private.

Module 04 · The Quant Process

The pipeline, one step at a time

QP 03 Thomas summarizes the whole workflow at the start of Episode 03. In order:

  1. Define an event QP 03. Pick the moments worth studying: a breakout, a sweep, a news release, a volatility spike. Not every bar.
  2. Measure outcomes QP 02. Decide what "worked" means: which barrier gets hit first, forward returns, or forward volatility.
  3. Add contextual features QP 02–04. The same event produces different outcomes. Find what explains the difference: time of day, relative volume, trend, volatility regime.
  4. Model the relationship. Use statistics and then machine learning to find which combinations of context predict which outcomes.
  5. Signal processing. Turn model output into trade decisions: thresholds and filters.
  6. Risk protocol. Place and manage bets: sizing, stops, exits (Module 10).
  7. Validate. Bootstrapping, Monte Carlo, out-of-sample testing, counting your trials (Module 08).
  8. Deploy and monitor. Only after validation. Compare live results to the expected range and retire the strategy when it decays (Module 09).

The key mindset shift: you test whether an idea has predictive power before you ever build a strategy around it. Most retail traders start at step 6 with someone else's entries and exits.

Module 05 · Quant Process 02

Feature engineering

QP 02 A feature is raw data collapsed into a number that captures something you care about. Raw candles (open, high, low, close) make a terrible model on their own. But every chart trader already uses features without calling them that. A trend line, ATR, RSI and relative volume are all transformations of the same OHLCV data.

"Your discretion is just a multi-dimensional pattern between features that you've engineered mentally but haven't actually quantified, and the outcomes of your trades."Thomas, QP 02

His airline analogy: to predict ticket prices from raw dates and temperatures, you'd naturally think "days until departure," "is it a holiday window?" and "how much warmer is the destination?" That's feature engineering. Once those are numbers, a computer can find the combinations far better than your intuition can.

How to build one: the logic tree

  1. What do I care about? Urgency, rejection, regime, distance, imbalance. Example: "Is the market trending or choppy?"
  2. What raw data holds traces of it? For trendiness, price alone is enough: trend efficiency, moving-average slope.
  3. What type should it be? Continuous (how efficient is the trend), binary (trending or not), or ordinal (−2 to +2). Try more than one and see which relates best to outcomes.
  4. What transformation makes the idea explicit? See the four transforms below.
  5. Visualize and validate. Plot examples and confirm the feature fires exactly when you meant it to. Thomas calls this extremely important.
normalize MA slope in % per bar, not $ per bar ratio ATR(short) / ATR(long) → "violent" breakout when ≫ 1 difference (price − SMA) / SMA → % stretch from the average encoding z = (stretch − mean) / std → how rare is this stretch?

Why normalize? A moving-average slope in dollars swings much more when the S&P is at 6,000 than when it was at 2,000, but the idea you care about (how steep is the trend) hasn't changed. Converting to percent keeps the feature's range stable across history, so a model doesn't mistake "the asset got more expensive" for "the trend got stronger."

Stationarity without amnesia: fractional differentiation

Models need inputs whose statistics don't drift, which is why prices get converted to returns. But returns throw away all memory of the price level. López de Prado's fix is to difference by a fraction:

Case study: sweeps of the prior day's high or low

QP 02 Thomas tested an idea popular with ICT traders on ES (S&P 500 futures). Event: price wicks beyond the previous day's high or low, then closes back inside. Outcome: an ATR-based double barrier, meaning which of an upper or lower level gets hit first.

  • 2023 only: the bearish case (high swept) had a 95% confidence interval for expected value that was entirely negative, so it was statistically significant. The bullish case's interval included zero.
  • 2022–2025, 512 events: the bearish interval now included zero. The "edge" from 2023 didn't hold over a longer period.

His conclusion: when one event leads to different outcomes in different periods, that's what contextual features are for. Mine, from Module 08: also suspect luck. A result that shows up in one year and fades with more data is the classic signature of an effect that was never there. Both explanations deserve testing.

The hidden problem: overlapping outcomes

Each event's outcome looks up to 10 days ahead, so events a day or two apart share most of their future. Consecutive outcomes on SPY have a correlation of 0.62 (0.44 two days apart, 0.19 five days apart). The √n in the formula assumes independent samples. Overlap means you have fewer than you think:

effective n ≈ n / (1 + 2·Σ ρₖ) ρₖ = correlation of outcomes k events apart true SE ≈ naive SE × √(n / effective n)

Resampling blocks of 10 consecutive events (a block bootstrap) widened the bearish confidence interval by about 30%, from [−0.07%, +0.18%] to [−0.11%, +0.22%]. Even the baseline's t of 5.44 is overstated. Fixes: block bootstrap, Newey-West standard errors, or only counting events whose outcome windows don't overlap.

Module 06 · Quant Process 03 · Lab

Event-defining features: stop sampling every candle

QP 03 An event-defining feature says: this timestamp is a decision point, include it in the experiment. If you try to predict the outcome of every bar, most bars have no catalyst, so you're forecasting noise and your accuracy will be terrible. Pick events where something happened, and the outcome may be learnable.

The tradeoff: events that fire too often are just noise again. Events that fire too rarely (once a year) don't give you enough samples to learn anything.

Event ideas from the episode

  • Price structure: breakout through a significant level, sweep of a prior session high or low, new day or week opening gap
  • Volatility: volatility spike, compression followed by expansion
  • Microstructure: order-book anomalies (Level 3 data)
  • Cross-asset: spread deviations between related instruments
  • News: earnings, CPI, FOMC

Common events like breakouts can still work, but then you need better context data or a better model than everyone else using them.

The CUSUM event detector

A classic in quant finance, and the example Thomas builds. Add up returns in two buckets, one for upward pressure and one for downward. When a bucket crosses a threshold, enough movement has piled up in one direction to count as an event. Flag it and reset that bucket.

S⁺ₜ = max(0, S⁺ₜ₋₁ + rₜ) S⁻ₜ = min(0, S⁻ₜ₋₁ + rₜ) event when S⁺ₜ > h or S⁻ₜ < −h, then reset that bucket to 0 h = k × current volatility (Thomas uses an ATR multiple)

Scaling the threshold by volatility matters. With a fixed threshold, a high-volatility regime spams you with events and a calm one gives you none. Try it:

Module 07

Models of price: Markov chains, Monte Carlo, Black-Scholes

"The Trillion Dollar Equation" traces the history. A few links to the rest of this course:

  • Ed Thorp, a physics PhD, invented card counting and bet more when the count favored him and less when it didn't. That's Kelly sizing (Module 10). He took the same thinking to markets and earned about 20% a year for 20 years.
  • Dynamic hedging. If Bob sells Alice a call option, he can offset it by holding Δ shares of the stock and adjusting as the price moves. The hedged position has minimal risk, so in effect he "manufactures" the option.
  • Black, Scholes & Merton (1973) argued that a perfectly hedged portfolio must earn the risk-free rate. That gave an explicit formula for option prices. The CBOE opened the same year, and derivatives markets are now several hundred trillion dollars.
  • Jim Simons founded Renaissance Technologies in 1978 and hired scientists, not finance people. One of them, Leonard Baum, pioneered hidden Markov models, which infer hidden states from what you can observe (Module 09). The Medallion fund became the best-performing fund ever.
  • The video's closing point matches Thomas's: once a pattern is found and exploited, it starts to disappear.

Markov chains: probability when events depend on each other

The Markov chain video explains the tool underneath regime models, simulation and the HMMs Renaissance used.

  • The law of large numbers says averages settle down as you collect more trials. Bernoulli proved it for independent events like coin flips. Nekrasov then argued backwards: if averages settle, the events must be independent.
  • Markov proved him wrong with vowels and consonants in Pushkin's Eugene Onegin. 43% of letters were vowels, so independent vowel-vowel pairs would be about 18%. He counted only 6%: letters depend on the previous one. Yet a chain built from those dependencies still settled to 43% vowels.
  • A Markov chain is a set of states plus transition probabilities between them. The next step depends only on the current state, not the full history ("memoryless"). Run it long enough and the share of time in each state converges to a stationary distribution.
  • Monte Carlo came from Ulam estimating his odds of winning solitaire by playing many games and counting. Von Neumann saw that neutrons, unlike solitaire deals, depend on their own history, so they simulated a Markov chain of events many times on ENIAC and read off the distribution of outcomes.
  • Where it breaks: systems with feedback loops. Markets have them: crowded trades unwinding together (the August 2007 quant crash), and models trained on data that the models themselves moved.

In trading, the states are usually regimes. Lab A in Module 06 is built on one: a two-state chain where a calm day turns volatile with 0.8% probability and a volatile day turns calm with 2.5% probability.

expected time in a state = 1 / P(leave) calm ≈ 125 days, volatile ≈ 40 days stationary share volatile = 0.008 / (0.008 + 0.025) ≈ 24%

Why this matters for testing. Trade outcomes are rarely independent. Losses cluster in bad regimes. Averages still converge, as Markov showed, but more slowly, so 500 clustered trades contain less information than 500 independent ones. Use a block bootstrap (resample runs of consecutive trades, not single trades) when you stress-test a backtest.

I tested when this matters. I simulated a strategy whose edge depends on a hidden regime: it gains 0.12% a day in the good state and loses 0.25% a day in the bad one. Over 10 years, its true median max drawdown is 41%. Resampling single days from one history (iid bootstrap) estimated 22%, about half. A stationary block bootstrap (Politis & Romano, random block lengths averaging 60 days) estimated 36%, much closer. The day-to-day correlation of that strategy's returns was only 0.06, so a tiny autocorrelation was enough to halve the drawdown estimate.

For plain SPY returns it barely matters. Real 2000–2026 max drawdown: 55%. iid bootstrap median: 47%. Block bootstrap: 45–51%. SPY's daily returns are nearly uncorrelated (lag-1: −0.08) even though their size is very persistent (correlation of absolute returns 0.28–0.33 out to 20 days). Volatility clustering alone doesn't break the iid bootstrap. A clustering edge does. Default to the block bootstrap anyway: it costs nothing.

The math you need

Geometric Brownian motion is the default model: the price drifts at rate μ and is shaken by noise proportional to its level, at volatility σ. This is "random movement plus a drift," which improved on Bachelier's earlier model.

dS = μ·S·dt + σ·S·dW S(t+Δt) = S(t) · exp((μ − σ²/2)Δt + σ·√Δt·Z), Z ~ N(0,1)

  • Tails follow a power law. The Hill estimator puts the loss-tail exponent at α ≈ 3 (2.8–3.2 depending on how many extremes you use), which matches the "inverse cubic law" found across markets. That implies the variance exists but the fourth moment doesn't, so sample kurtosis is unstable and driven by a handful of days.
  • A single fat-tailed distribution overcorrects. A Student-t fit (ν ≈ 2.5) predicts 27 days beyond 10σ, when there were 5. The real cause is mostly volatility switching between calm and turbulent regimes (Module 09): a mixture of normals with different volatilities is fat-tailed.
  • Longer horizons are less fat-tailed but more skewed. Kurtosis falls from 20 at 1 day to 7 at 3 months, while skew goes from −0.46 to −0.84. Crashes are a multi-day phenomenon.

Modeling changing volatility: EWMA and GARCH

EWMA (RiskMetrics) σ²ₜ = λ·σ²ₜ₋₁ + (1−λ)·r²ₜ₋₁ λ = 0.94 GARCH(1,1) σ²ₜ = ω + α·r²ₜ₋₁ + β·σ²ₜ₋₁ persistence α+β, shock half-life = ln(½)/ln(α+β), long-run variance = ω/(1−α−β) GJR (asymmetric) add γ·r²ₜ₋₁ when yesterday was a down day

Fitted to the CRSP market, 1926–2026:

  • Volatility is extremely persistent. α = 0.10, β = 0.90, α+β = 0.997, so a volatility shock takes about 216 trading days to decay by half. That's why a calm year tells you little about next month once a shock hits.
  • Volatility clustering explains most of the fat tails. Dividing each day's return by GARCH's volatility forecast cuts kurtosis from 20 to 8.5, and days beyond 5σ from 104 to 31. The remainder is true jumps, like 1987.
  • Down moves drive volatility; up moves barely do. An asymmetric GJR fit on 1990–2026 gives α = 0.001 for up-shocks and α+γ = 0.175 for down-shocks. This is the "leverage effect," and it's why the market's return distribution is skewed left.
  • Better, not perfect. In the same 99% VaR backtest, refitted each year with no look-ahead, GARCH with t-distributed shocks had 134 breaches against 90 expected. EWMA with a normal quantile had 183. GARCH still fails the Kupiec test, but it's the best of the five methods.

Getting more out of each daily bar: range-based volatility

Parkinson σ² = (ln H/L)² / (4 ln 2) Garman-Klass σ² = ½(ln H/L)² − (2 ln 2 − 1)(ln C/O)² Rogers-Satchell σ² = ln(H/C)·ln(H/O) + ln(L/C)·ln(L/O) (unaffected by drift) Yang-Zhang overnight variance + weighted open-to-close + Rogers-Satchell

The high and low contain information that the close throws away. In simulation, Parkinson was 5.2× more efficient than close-to-close (matching theory), and Garman-Klass about 8×. One day's high-low range tells you as much as a week of closes.

On real SPY, 1999–2026, there's a catch. Close-to-close volatility was 19.2%, Parkinson 15.8% and Garman-Klass 16.0%. The gap is overnight moves: 34% of SPY's variance happens between the close and the next open, and the high-low range never sees it. Adding the overnight gap back fixes this. Forecasting next month's variance from the past month, Garman-Klass plus overnight beat plain close-to-close by about 17% (QLIKE loss 0.391 vs 0.473), using the same daily bars.

Itô's lemma: where the −σ²/2 comes from

Ordinary calculus drops second-order terms. Random motion can't, because a Brownian step of size √dt squared is dt, the same order as the drift. The rules:

(dW)² = dt dW·dt = 0 (dt)² = 0 df = f′(S)·dS + ½·f″(S)·(dS)² ← Itô's lemma for f = ln S: d ln S = (μ − ½σ²)dt + σ·dW

I checked the first rule numerically. Splitting one unit of time into 100,000 random steps, the sum of squared steps came to 0.9994 (theory: exactly 1). The sum of absolute steps came to 252 and keeps growing. Brownian paths are infinitely jagged but have a well-defined squared variation, and that's what makes Itô calculus work.

What it means for your money: the average outcome grows at μ, but the typical (median) outcome grows at μ − ½σ². The ½σ² is volatility drag. With μ = 8% and σ = 40% over 10 years, 200,000 simulated paths had a mean of 2.23× (theory e^0.8 = 2.23), but a median of exactly 1.00×. Half the paths lost money despite a positive expected return.

GBM is itself a Markov process: tomorrow's price depends only on today's. Simulating thousands of GBM paths is Ulam's Monte Carlo method applied to prices. Thomas used Monte Carlo to tune a prop-firm strategy, and QuantPad uses it (plus bootstrapping) to show the range of outcomes a backtest could have produced. The term MCMC (Markov chain Monte Carlo), from his TikTok title, usually means something more specific: building a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is a probability distribution you want to sample, such as the uncertainty in a model's μ and σ.

Mean reversion uses the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, which pulls a value back toward its mean at speed θ. It's the math behind pairs and stat-arb: Avellaneda & Lee traded each stock's "s-score," opening at |s| > 1.25, closing longs at −0.50 and shorts at 0.75.

dX = θ(μ − X)dt + σ·dW half-life = ln(2) / θ s-score = (X − μ) / σ_eq

Estimating mean reversion, and how it fools you

Sampled at a fixed step Δ, an OU process is exactly a first-order autoregression. So you estimate it with a simple regression of tomorrow's value on today's:

X(t+1) = a + b·X(t) + ε b = e^(−θΔ) → θ = −ln(b)/Δ, mean μ = a/(1−b), half-life = ln 2 / θ

The trap: in short samples, b comes out biased low, so mean reversion looks faster than it really is. I simulated a process with a true half-life of 43.7 trading days. With 1 year of data the median estimate was 22 days, half the truth, and 10% of estimates were under 11 days. With 3 years it was 34 days, and with 10 years 41. A spread that seems to snap back in two weeks on one year of data may really take two months, and that's the difference between a trade that works and one that bleeds while you wait.

Cointegration: what pairs trading actually needs

Two prices that each wander like random walks are cointegrated if some combination of them, y − β·x, stays stationary and mean-reverts. That spread is what a pairs trader trades. The Engle-Granger test regresses y on x, then checks the residual for stationarity using special critical values.

Never trust a regression of one price on another. I regressed pairs of completely independent random walks (500 points, 1,000 trials). The slope was "significant" (|t| > 1.96) in 90% of trials, with a median R² of 0.18. This is Granger & Newbold's "spurious regression" (1974). Trending series always look related.

Beyond stocks: the yield curve's three factors

Principal components of daily changes in Treasury yields (FRED: 3-month, 1-, 2-, 5-, 10- and 30-year, 1990–2026, 9,199 days) show that three movements explain 96% of everything the curve does:

  • Level (75.6%): all yields move together.
  • Slope (14.2%): short and long yields move in opposite directions, so the curve steepens or flattens.
  • Curvature (6.6%): the 2–5-year middle moves against both ends.

This matches Litterman & Scheinkman (1991), and it's the fixed-income version of the "only a few eigenvalues are real" result in Module 10. A bond's sensitivity to the level factor is its duration. For a 10-year 4% bond, modified duration is 8.11 and convexity 80.8. A 300 basis-point rise costs exactly −21.1%. Duration alone predicts −24.3%, and adding convexity (½·C·Δy²) gives −20.7%.

Interest-rate models: Vasicek and CIR

Vasicek dr = κ(θ − r)dt + σ·dW (an OU process for rates; can go negative) CIR dr = κ(θ − r)dt + σ·√r·dW (stays positive if 2κθ ≥ σ²) zero-coupon bond (Vasicek): P = A·e^(−B·r₀), B = (1 − e^(−κT))/κ

Fitted to the 3-month T-bill (FRED, weekly, 1981–2026), Vasicek gives a long-run rate of 2.76% with a 2.7-year half-life, and a 4.2% chance of negative rates. CIR passes the Feller condition (0.0047 ≥ 0.0038), so it stays positive. The bond formula is exact: 0.81488 by closed form and 0.81488 by Monte Carlo (200,000 paths). But fitted to 2009–2026 alone, Vasicek's mean-reversion speed turns negative. Years at zero followed by a hiking cycle don't look like a mean-reverting process at all. Rate models are useful tools whose parameters break across policy regimes.

Two lessons. First, the speed of mean reversion itself changes: VIX snaps back twice as fast since 2020 as it did before 2008. Second, a mean-reverting index isn't automatically a trade. You can't buy VIX itself, and VIX futures already price in the expected reversion.

Option greeks: Delta is the hedge ratio (option move per $1 stock move). Gamma is how fast delta changes. Theta is time decay. Hedgers who are long gamma make money re-hedging and pay for it in theta. Selling options collects theta in exchange for gamma risk: it feels like steady income until a big move.

Breeden-Litzenberger: risk-neutral density q(K) = e^(rT) · ∂²C/∂K²

Differentiating call prices twice across strikes recovers the probability distribution the market is pricing. From this chain it has a skew of −1.42. That's how professionals use Black-Scholes: as a quoting convention (prices expressed in volatility units), not a model of the world. Models that produce a smile add stochastic volatility (Heston), jumps (Merton, Bates) or volatility that depends on price level (Dupire's local vol).

How uncertain is a Sharpe ratio? The math

A Sharpe ratio is an estimate: the average return divided by its standard deviation. Its standard error (Lo 2002; Mertens 2002 for non-normal returns), with SR measured per period and T periods:

Var(SR̂) ≈ ( 1 + ½·SR² − skew·SR + (kurt − 3)/4 · SR² ) / T annualized: SE(SR_annual) ≈ 1 / √(years of data)

The second line is the key fact. It holds because per-day Sharpe ratios are tiny, so the SR² terms nearly vanish and the √252 factors cancel. Sampling more often doesn't help. Only more calendar time shrinks the uncertainty. Five years of data means your annual Sharpe is ±0.45 at one standard error, whether you use daily or minute bars. I checked this by simulation: 4,000 five-year daily samples gave a standard error of 0.0282 per day, matching the formula exactly.

That's the Minimum Track Record Length: MinTRL = 1 + (1 − skew·SR + (kurt−1)/4·SR²) × (1.645 / SR)², in periods. Real SPY, 1999–2026: annual Sharpe 0.44 ± 0.19, with skew −0.20 and kurtosis 14.3. Even the stock market itself needed about 14 years to clear the bar.

  • When skew and fat tails matter: at low frequency with a high Sharpe. A monthly strategy with an annual Sharpe of 1.7, skew −3 and kurtosis 20 (typical of option selling) has a standard error 1.8× larger than the normal formula says. The Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio builds this in: PSR = Φ[(SR̂ − SR*)·√(T−1) / √(1 − skew·SR̂ + (kurt−1)/4·SR̂²)].
  • When returns are autocorrelated (smoothed or stale prices, slow-moving positions), the usual √12 or √252 annualization overstates the Sharpe. Lo's correction: SR_annual = SR × q/√(q + 2Σ(q−k)ρₖ). On a simulated series with lag-1 correlation 0.5, the naive annual Sharpe of 1.79 was really 1.31.

Ranking by past Sharpe mostly ranks luck: James-Stein shrinkage

shrunk estimate = average + B·(own estimate − average), B = max(0, 1 − noise variance / spread variance)

When you compare many strategies or assets, part of the spread in their Sharpe ratios is just noise, about 1/years of variance each (from the Sharpe error formula above). I estimated each of the 30 Ken French industries' Sharpe ratios on 1927–1976. The spread across industries (variance 0.008) was smaller than pure noise would produce (0.020), so the optimal shrinkage keeps 0% of each industry's difference from the average. Checking against 1977–2026: the raw first-half Sharpes predicted the second half worse than simply using the average (error 0.0525 vs 0.0502), and the two halves correlated only 0.22. Fifty years of data couldn't tell the industries apart. Before you pick the "best" strategy from a list, shrink toward the group average.

What the literature says about this problem

  • Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016). Hundreds of factors have been data-mined, so a new one needs a t-statistic above 3.0, not the usual 2.0. They conclude "most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false."
  • Hou, Xue & Zhang (2020). Replicated 447 published anomalies: 64% were insignificant at the 5% level, and 85% failed the t > 3 hurdle.
  • Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen (2023) disagree. With a Bayesian approach, most factors do replicate, fall into 13 themes, and work in 93 countries. The robust effects are real. The weak, narrow ones are noise.
  • Deflated Sharpe Ratio. Corrects a backtest's Sharpe for the number of trials, return length, skew and fat tails. Probability of Backtest Overfitting (CSCV) estimates how often your in-sample winner ranks below median out of sample.

Your backtest checklist

  1. Write down every variant you try. The trial count is part of the result.
  2. Hold out data you never touch until the end. Test it once.
  3. No look-ahead: signals use only data available before the trade. Watch for revised data and close prices you couldn't actually trade at.
  4. Include delisted and bankrupt stocks, so there's no survivorship bias.
  5. Use purged and embargoed cross-validation when labels overlap in time.
  6. Compare against a random-entry baseline over the same period (QP 03).
  7. Charge realistic costs, then double them and see if the edge survives.
  8. Check robustness: nearby parameters should work almost as well. A lone spike is a fluke.
  9. Make sure the edge has an economic reason (Module 03).

Regimes

Markets switch between states such as calm uptrend, choppy range and crisis. A hidden Markov model, the tool Leonard Baum brought to Renaissance, is a Markov chain of regimes you can't see directly (Module 07). It estimates which state you're probably in from returns and volatility, and how likely each switch is. Thomas's volatility-regime and trend features (QP 02) are simpler versions of the same idea. Use regime estimates to size or switch off a strategy, not as a crystal ball: they're only known with confidence after the fact.

Using it to scale position size did slightly better than switching in and out.

Value's lost decade and a half

The value factor (Ken French HML, 1926–2026, Sharpe 0.35) peaked in December 2006, then fell 56% to September 2020. As of August 2026 it still hasn't regained that peak. Before 2007 its worst drawdown had been 44%, in 1935.

Was 56% "impossible"? Resampling value's own pre-2007 history in 2-year blocks over 100-year paths, a drawdown that deep showed up 13% of the time. Extreme, but not inconsistent with its past. A normal-distribution calculation says a Sharpe-0.46 strategy loses over 14 years only 4% of the time, which understates it. Even a century-old risk premium can go nowhere for 14+ years. Size your expectations from block bootstraps of the strategy's own history, not textbook formulas, and decide in advance what would make you quit (the table below).

Bayesian evaluation: how sure are you, and when should you quit?

win rate posterior: Beta(1 + wins, 1 + losses) (flat prior) SPRT: add ln[P(trade | no edge) / P(trade | edge)] after each trade; stop at ±ln((1−α)/α)
  • 55 wins in 100 even-payoff trades gives only an 84% chance the true win rate beats 50% (95% interval 45–64%). 550 of 1,000 gives 99.9%.
  • The prior matters. Most strategies have no edge. With a skeptical prior (win rate within about ±1 point of 50%), 55 of 100 moves you to just 57.8% confidence, and even 550 of 1,000 only to 95.5%.
  • Wald's sequential probability ratio test answers "has my 55% edge decayed to nothing?" In simulation it took a median of 434 trades to decide either way (96% correct, 4% false alarms). Telling a real 5-point edge from none takes hundreds of trades. Set the stopping rule before you start.

Position sizing: Kelly and volatility targeting

Even a real edge can ruin you if you bet too big. Thorp's blackjack rule, bet more when the odds favor you, is formalized by the Kelly criterion: the bet size that maximizes long-run growth. For a continuous return stream with excess return μ and volatility σ:

f* = μ / σ² growth(f) = f·μ − ½·f²·σ²
Lab C · Growth vs. bet sizeannualized
Full Kelly leverage–
Growth at full Kelly–
Growth at half Kelly–
Growth at 2× Kelly–

Three things to notice:

  • Half Kelly keeps 75% of the growth with half the volatility. Most professionals bet at or below half Kelly.
  • At 2× Kelly, growth drops to zero. Bet more than that and you lose money despite having an edge.
  • Your μ is an estimate, probably inflated by overfitting (Module 08). If you overestimate your edge, full Kelly is actually over-betting, which is another reason to bet a fraction.

The Kelly math, and a century of evidence

discrete bets: maximize p·ln(1+f·b) + q·ln(1−f) ⇒ f* = p − q/b continuous: g(L) = r + L(μ−r) − ½L²σ² ⇒ L* = (μ−r)/σ², g* = r + ½·Sharpe² many assets: w* = Σ⁻¹(μ − r) ← the Markowitz tangency portfolio, scaled drawdown law: P(ever falling to fraction x of your money) = x^(2/c − 1), c = fraction of Kelly

A 55% coin at even odds: bet 10% of your bankroll. The drawdown law is the one to memorize. At full Kelly there's a 50% chance you'll be cut in half at some point, and a 25% chance you'll lose three-quarters. At half Kelly those drop to 12.5% and 1.6%. I checked by simulation (3,000 paths, 50 years): full Kelly gave 49% and 23%, half Kelly 11% and 1%.

I also tested it on the real CRSP market, 1926–2026, at constant daily-rebalanced leverage, generously assuming you could borrow at the T-bill rate:

The Itô growth formula matches a century of real compounding almost exactly. But look at the drawdowns: the growth-optimal leverage meant watching 99.5% of your money disappear in 1929–32. No person or fund survives that. Worse, the inputs aren't knowable in advance. Estimating Kelly from the trailing 10 years gave anything from 0× to 15.5× depending on the year. That's why practitioners treat half Kelly as a ceiling, not a target.

Research Volatility targeting scales each position by 1/σ so every position carries the same risk. Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen sized every futures position to 40% annualized volatility. Moreira & Muir showed that cutting size when volatility spikes improved Sharpe ratios across many factors.

I replicated their market result on CRSP data, 1926–2026. Each month the position is sized by 1 ÷ last month's realized variance, then scaled to match the market's overall volatility:

Cut size when volatility spikes. Don't lever up 15× when it's quiet.

The optimizer looks like a 0.90 Sharpe in hindsight, then delivers 0.23. That matches DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2009): naive 1/N is very hard to beat. The culprit is expected returns. Means are estimated so poorly (Module 08: a Sharpe needs years to pin down) that the optimizer mostly bets on noise, then inverting Σ magnifies it into 10× leverage. What works is ignoring expected returns (minimum variance) and shrinking the covariance.

How much of a correlation matrix is real? Random matrix theory gives the answer. For N assets and T observations of pure noise, eigenvalues fall between (1 ± √(N/T))². On the 30 industries' last two years of daily data, that noise band tops out at 1.55. Only 3 of 30 eigenvalues exceed it. The biggest (12.3) is the market itself, explaining 41% of all variance. The other 27 dimensions are statistically indistinguishable from noise. A pure-noise control with the same N and T gave a maximum of 1.47, matching the theory. That's why practitioners clip small eigenvalues before inverting a covariance matrix.

A bug worth remembering. My first equal-risk solver used a simple iterative update that returns NaN when an asset's risk contribution is negative, which happens with negative correlations. Prop-firm challenges

QP 01 Thomas treats funded-account challenges as "a financial carnival game": hit a profit target before a loss limit. Kelly doesn't apply directly because the payoff isn't long-run growth. It's a pass/fail bet with a fee. He says he used Monte Carlo simulation to tune a strategy with a 93% modeled chance of passing a Topstep challenge, then passed his first one on the first try. That's a good use of simulation. Keep in mind that one pass is a single sample, and 93% is only as good as the backtest it was resampled from.

The math of passing a challenge

A challenge is a Brownian motion between two barriers: a profit target +a and a loss limit −b. With daily drift μ and volatility σ, the probability of hitting the target first has a closed form (gambler's ruin with drift):

P(pass) = (1 − e^(2μb/σ²)) / (e^(−2μa/σ²) − e^(2μb/σ²)) no edge (μ = 0): P = b / (a + b)

With a +6% target and a −4% limit, checked against 20,000 simulated paths per case:

TraderPass, fixed loss limitPass, trailing drawdownMedian days
No edge40%29%28
Paying costs, no edge29%20%28
Paying costs, 3× position size36%31%4
Sharpe 1, 1% daily volatility55%43%27
Sharpe 2, 1% daily volatility69%56%24
Sharpe 2, 0.5% daily volatility87%73%68
  • A coin-flipper passes 40% of the time. Passing proves very little. That's why the firms can sell challenges profitably.
  • With no edge, bigger size raises your odds. A trader paying costs passes 29% of the time at normal size but 36% at 3× size. This is "bold play" (Dubins & Savage): when the odds are against you, fewer, bigger bets are better. It's why gamblers swinging huge positions sometimes pass, and why firms add consistency rules.
  • With a real edge, smaller size raises your odds, but it takes longer.
  • A trailing drawdown costs 11–16 points of pass probability.
  • Reaching 93% with a fixed limit takes an annual Sharpe of 5.2 at 1% daily volatility, 2.6 at 0.5% (about 60 days), or 1.3 at 0.25% (about 227 days). Thomas's figure is achievable with a real edge, small size and patience, but not otherwise.

Break-even: with fee F and pass probability P, your expected payout per pass must exceed F/P. For a $150 challenge that's $375 at a 40% pass rate and $161 at 93%.

Small edges die from costs. Take a 55% win rate with equal-sized wins and losses (1R), and add a cost per side as a fraction of R:

On a tight-stop scalp, spread plus slippage of 5% of your stop distance wipes out a 55% win rate entirely. The shorter your holding period, the bigger costs are relative to the edge.

  • Research Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz used about $1.7 trillion of AQR's real trades. Size, value and momentum survive costs at large scale. Short-term reversal does not.
  • Almgren & Chriss describe the execution tradeoff. Trading fast pays more market impact. Trading slow leaves you exposed to price moves. The optimal schedule sits on an "efficient frontier" between the two.
  • Pairs-trading profits shrank once bid-ask bounce was accounted for. Always ask whether a backtest's fill price was actually tradable.

The math of trading big: Almgren-Chriss

minimize E[cost] + λ·Var[cost] selling X shares over time T optimal holdings x(t) = X · sinh(κ(T−t)) / sinh(κT), κ = √(λσ²/η) λ → 0: sell evenly (TWAP) large λ: sell fast up front to cut price risk

η is the temporary impact per unit of trading speed, and σ is the asset's volatility. I solved the problem numerically for 50 trading intervals, and the result matched the closed-form sinh path to within 0.004%. Along the efficient frontier, in one example, paying an extra $1.06M in impact bought a $144k reduction in the standard deviation of the outcome. Every desk chooses where on that curve to sit.

The square-root law of market impact

price impact ≈ Y · σ_daily · √( order size / average daily dollar volume ), Y ≈ 0.5–1

This is one of the most robust empirical laws in finance (Tóth et al. 2011; Bouchaud et al. 2018). It holds across stocks, futures, eras and countries. Impact grows with the square root of size, so a 100× bigger order costs about 10× more per share. Using the last 60 days of real volume and volatility:

For a retail-sized trade in a liquid market, impact is essentially zero. Your costs are the spread, fees and slippage from slow execution. Impact is what limits capacity: why a strategy that works with $100k can fail with $100M.

Market making: Avellaneda-Stoikov

reservation price r = mid − q·γ·σ²·(T−t) q = your inventory total spread δ_ask + δ_bid = γσ²(T−t) + (2/γ)·ln(1 + γ/k)

A market maker earns the spread but gets stuck with inventory when prices move. The fix is to shade both quotes against your position: long inventory, quote lower to sell it off. I re-ran the paper's simulation with its exact parameters over 1,000 paths. Inventory-shaded quoting earned 64.9 ± 6.8 with an inventory standard deviation of 2.8. Symmetric quoting around the mid earned 66.8 ± 13.4 with an inventory standard deviation of 7.9. The paper reports 62.9 ± 5.9 and 67.2 ± 13.4. Shading by inventory halves the risk for about 3% less profit.

Why spreads exist: trading against people who know more

Glosten-Milgrom: ask = E[V | someone buys], bid = E[V | someone sells] initial spread = π · (V_high − V_low) π = share of informed traders Kyle: price = λ · order flow, λ = σ_v / (2σ_u) ("Kyle's lambda" = market depth)
  • Glosten-Milgrom. With 30% informed traders and a $2 range of possible values, the spread starts at exactly $0.60. Each trade carries information, so the dealer updates. In my simulation (2,000 runs) the spread shrank to $0.29 after 10 trades and $0.09 after 30, while the price moved toward the true value. The spread is what the dealer charges everyone to cover losses to the informed.
  • Kyle. An insider who knows the true value trades β = σ_u/σ_v units per dollar of mispricing and hides inside the noise traders' orders. I simulated 400,000 rounds: the price-impact slope came out at 1.00 (theory 1.00), exactly half the insider's information leaked into the price (0.500), and the insider's profit was 1.002 (theory 1.000). Trading twice as aggressively drove the insider's profit to about zero.

For you: when your orders fill instantly at good prices, ask who's on the other side. Fills that go against you right away are the sign of adverse selection.

A model that fails on real data: Roll's (1984) spread estimator, s = 2√(−Cov(Δpₜ, Δpₜ₋₁)), applied to two years of daily SPY prices, says the spread is 54 basis points. The real spread is about 0.2. The estimator assumes bid-ask bounce is the only source of negative autocorrelation in price changes, but daily SPY has short-term reversal (Module 08). When a model's assumptions break, its output is meaningless.

Module 12

Machine learning: what it really buys you

QP 02 Thomas's plan is to engineer features, then let machine learning find the "multi-dimensional, nonlinear" relationships between context and outcomes. The research supports that, with realistic expectations. Research The benchmark study is Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020):

  • Trees and neural networks did best because they capture interactions between predictors that linear models miss. That's exactly what Thomas means by "multi-dimensional."
  • Monthly out-of-sample R² for individual stocks was only 0.33–0.40%. Predictability is tiny, but across thousands of stocks it was enough to double some strategies' performance.
  • Every method found the same dominant signals: momentum, liquidity and volatility. ML found better ways to combine known effects, not secret new ones.

The Fundamental Law of Active Management

IR ≈ IC × √breadth IC = correlation of your forecast with what happens breadth = independent bets per year

Grinold's law explains why a tiny predictive edge can be valuable. In simulation it's exact: an IC of 0.02 across 3,000 independent stocks a month gave an information ratio of 3.76, against a predicted 3.79. An IC of 0.05 on 30 assets gave 0.94 against 0.95. The 0.3–0.4% R² in Gu, Kelly & Xiu corresponds to an IC around 0.06, which is useful across thousands of stocks.

In practice it overpromises. Industry momentum on the 30 Ken French industries (1927–2026) has a mean rank IC of 0.067, so the law predicts IR = 0.067 × √(30 × 12) = 1.27. The real long-short Sharpe was 0.49. There are two reasons. The IC swings wildly from month to month (standard deviation 0.31), so a better estimate is mean IC ÷ std IC × √12 = 0.74. And the 30 industries are highly correlated, so they're nowhere near 30 independent bets. Measure your signal's IC over time, and treat IC × √N as an upper bound.

Be wary of any claim of 70%+ accuracy predicting tomorrow's direction. Real, publishable predictability is closer to a coin flip with a slight tilt. Very high accuracy almost always means look-ahead bias or overlapping labels leaking into the test set.

A good fit for Thomas's event framework is meta-labeling (López de Prado): your event rule proposes the trade and its direction, and a model trained on contextual features decides only whether to take it and how big.

Your practice path

Do these in order. Each one teaches you a skill the next one needs. Python with pandas, numpy and free data (yfinance for daily ETFs and crypto) covers all of them. If you'd rather not code, this is the workflow QuantPad automates, so you can use it there instead.

  1. Simulate before you trade. Generate GBM paths and a random walk. Recreate Lab B and confirm a "great" backtest appears in noise.
  2. Write a strategy spec. Pick one idea and write rules exact enough for a stranger to trade without asking you anything (Thomas's minimum bar).
  3. Replicate QP 03. Code a volatility-scaled CUSUM on SPY or BTC daily data. Sweep k, count events, and plot 1/5/20-day forward returns next to a random-day baseline.
  4. Replicate QP 02. Prior-day high/low sweeps with ATR double-barrier outcomes. Compute expected value with 95% confidence intervals, first for one year, then for five. See whether the result holds.
  5. Add three contextual features using the logic tree: one continuous, one binary, one ordinal, all normalized. Split events by each and log every split you test.
  6. Walk-forward and deflate. Fit on early years, test later ones, roll forward. Compute the Deflated Sharpe Ratio using your trial count.
  7. Paper trade for three months. Compare live fills to backtest assumptions. The gap is your real cost.

Check yourself

What's the difference between an event-defining and a contextual feature?

An event-defining feature picks which bars are in the experiment, such as a sweep of yesterday's high. A contextual feature explains why the same event has different outcomes, such as the volatility regime or time of day when it happened.

Why normalize a moving-average slope to percent?

In dollars, the slope gets bigger just because the asset got more expensive. The idea you care about, how steep the trend is, hasn't changed. Percent keeps the feature comparable across history.

Bitcoin is up 20 days after your CUSUM events. Is that an edge?

Not yet. Compare against random days in the same period. In 2024 Bitcoin rose so much that random days looked just as good. The event only matters if it beats the baseline.

You tried 200 parameter combos and the best has a Sharpe of 1.2 over 5 years. Impressive?

No. Luck alone gives an expected best of about 1.2 with 100–200 independent tries. You need the Deflated Sharpe Ratio, a held-out test, or evidence that neighboring parameters also work.

Your edge estimate is 8% a year with 20% vol. What leverage would you actually use?

Full Kelly is 2×. Since the 8% is probably overstated, half Kelly (1×) or less is sensible. You'd keep about 75% of the growth with half the risk.

A strategy worked great 2010–2018 and has been flat since it was posted online. Why?

Edge decay. Published or popular signals lose about half their returns once people trade them (McLean & Pontiff). Part of the original result may also have been overfitting.

Your backtest's trade results average out nicely over 500 trades. Does that mean the trades were independent?

No. That was Nekrasov's mistake. Markov showed that dependent events also converge. If your losses cluster in certain regimes, your 500 trades carry less information than 500 independent ones, so use a block bootstrap and expect larger drawdowns.

Black-Scholes doesn't use the stock's expected return. Why not?

A delta-hedged option position is riskless over small moves, so it must earn the risk-free rate no matter what the stock's drift is. Only volatility matters.

Prediction markets

A prediction-market contract pays $1 if an event happens and $0 if not, so its price is a probability. Polymarket runs a central limit order book settled in USDC. Kalshi is a CFTC-regulated exchange. The quant questions are the same as anywhere else: are the prices calibrated, where are they biased, and can you trade the bias after costs?

Scoring forecasts properly

Brier = −(r − y)² log score = ln r_outcome both "proper": truthful reporting maximizes expected score

A scoring rule is proper if your best strategy is to report what you actually believe. With a true probability of 70%, both Brier and log scores are maximized by reporting exactly 0.70. A naive "linear" score (r if YES, 1 − r if NO) is maximized by reporting 0.99, which rewards exaggeration. On the Polymarket data, the log-loss skill is 38% versus 45% for Brier, because log loss punishes confident misses hard: a contract at 0.6¢ that won, one at 97¢ that lost. If you trade prediction markets, track your log loss.

Market-maker math: Hanson's LMSR

cost function C(q) = b · ln Σᵢ e^(qᵢ/b) price of outcome i = e^(qᵢ/b) / Σⱼ e^(qⱼ/b) cost of a trade = C(q_after) − C(q_before) worst-case market-maker loss = b · ln(n)

The logarithmic market scoring rule is the log score turned into a market: a trader who moves the price from p₀ to p₁ earns exactly b·[ln p₁ − ln p₀] on the outcome that happens. I checked this: 0.5 → 0.9 pays 58.779 = 100·ln(0.9/0.5). It's the classic automated market maker for prediction markets. Prices are a softmax, so they always sum to exactly $1. The liquidity parameter b sets depth: with b = 1,000, moving a binary from 50% to 80% takes 1,386 YES shares costing $916. The market maker's maximum loss is b·ln(n): $693 for a binary, $4,852 for a 128-outcome market. A trader who knows the true probability p and pushes the price there costs the market maker exactly b × KL(p ‖ 0.5). I verified this numerically (e.g., $192.74 at p = 0.8). So the market maker is paying for information, and b is the size of the subsidy. Polymarket itself now runs a limit-order book, where the inventory-skewed quoting of Module 11 applies.

I simulated a market maker quoting a two-week binary contract. The true probability drifts, the market maker's estimate lags the news by 6 hours, and a share of incoming orders are informed: they trade only when the quote is wrong.

Informed flow eats the spread, so the more informed traders, the wider the spread needed to break even (Glosten-Milgrom from Module 11). The profit's standard deviation was about ±12 contracts' worth in every case, because it's dominated by whatever inventory is left when the contract jumps to $0 or $1. In reality, competing market makers push spreads down toward break-even.

Polymarket also pays makers directly. Its liquidity rewards score each resting order as S = ((v − s)/v)² · b, where s is the distance from the midpoint and v is the market's maximum qualifying spread. Rewards are boosted for quoting both sides, sampled every minute, and paid daily (per Polymarket's docs). Because the score is quadratic, it strongly rewards tight quotes, so rewards can make a slightly adverse-selected book profitable.

Putting the measured dynamics to work. I re-ran a market-making simulation with fair values that follow the volatility law measured below (largest near 50%, growing toward expiry) plus occasional 20–50¢ jumps. Three quoting rules were compared over 400 thirty-day markets. A fixed 1¢ spread earned $10 per market with a $158 standard deviation. Scaling the spread with volatility cut the risk to $135. Also skewing quotes against inventory (lowering both quotes when long, raising them when short) cut it to $43 and tripled the return per unit of risk. Jumps cost about $4 a market in stale quotes picked off. For a binary market maker, managing inventory comes first, because whatever you hold at resolution pays $0 or $1.

Kalshi's Liquidity Incentive Program: paid to rest orders

Kalshi pays U.S. members for resting orders that deepen the book, even if they never fill. It's published in CFTC filings and the help center, and currently runs to January 1, 2027. Every second, at a random moment, Kalshi takes a snapshot of each incentivized market. The snapshot counts only if both the YES and NO sides hold at least the Target Size (live values 300–2,500 contracts). For each side:

walk down from the best bid, whole price levels, until cumulative size ≥ Target → qualifying bids reference = first level where cumulative size ≥ Target/5 score(bid) = DF^(ticks below reference) × size (DF ≈ 0.4–0.5: one tick back = half credit) your snapshot score = your share of YES-side score + your share of NO-side score payout = (your Σ scores ÷ everyone's Σ) × period reward × (counted snapshots ÷ all snapshots)

On October 15, 2026, Kalshi's public API listed 5,734 markets with live programs at that moment. Rebuilding the history from all 319,000 programs gives the true total: about $290,000 a day over the past week ($268,000 a day over 30 days, peaking at $398,000), across roughly 10,000 markets a day. About 60% of that money is in programs lasting an hour or a day (hourly Miami temperature, 15-minute crypto and currency contracts, NFL stat ladders), which are created continuously. Pools have grown about 30-fold in a year, from about $250,000 a month in late 2025 to $5.9 million in September 2026. Polymarket's comparable rewards cover 18,649 markets and total about $210,000 a day. The median pool is only $14 a day per market. The richest pools are hourly Miami-temperature contracts, Rotten Tomatoes scores and state gas-price markets. I simulated a newcomer joining the best bid on both sides of the 250 richest books. Holding everything else still, they'd take a 4–34% share and earn roughly 2–15% of their capital per day, before any losses from getting filled.

That's an upper bound, for three reasons. Other providers re-quote, which competes the reward down toward its costs. A resting best bid gets filled exactly when the price is moving against it, worst in hourly weather contracts where takers watch live thermometers. And fills become 0-or-$1 positions at resolution. The program subsidizes depth, not tightness, so the real opportunity is market making plus rewards: quote both sides, skew against inventory, pull quotes before news, and size so rewards exceed measured fill losses. The next step is a paper trial on logged order books, now built into the home-PC capture script.

Kalshi vs Polymarket rewards. Polymarket scores each order by its distance from the midpoint, quadratically: credit = ((v − s)/v)², zero beyond a max spread v of a few cents. It also takes the smaller of each provider's two sides, so a provider has to quote both. Kalshi scores distance from the book's own reference level, geometrically (half credit per tick back). It also only needs the market as a whole to be two-sided. So Polymarket pays for quoting tight around fair value, while Kalshi pays for being at the top of whatever book exists. In a thin Kalshi book, a wide quote can still earn full credit.

Where the rewards go. A proportional reward split is a classic contest. With n providers each choosing depth, the equilibrium is:

total depth Q* = R(n−1)/(n·k) profit per provider = R/n² return on cost = 1/(n−1) free entry (n → ∞): R = k·Q — the whole subsidy turns into extra depth

Here k is the all-in cost per contract-day. A numerical best-response check matched the formulas exactly. If live Kalshi books were in free-entry equilibrium, their depth would imply k ≈ 11¢ per contract per day, about 800 times the cost of capital alone. So depth is limited by fill risk or by how few providers compete, not by money. That's good news for a careful newcomer, and a warning about where the real cost lies.

Measuring that cost. I took every public fill in the 51 richest long-running reward markets over 36 hours, about 442,000 contracts, and marked each against the midpoint afterwards. The resting side lost 2.5¢ per contract within 5 minutes, and 3.4¢ within an hour, even after earning the 1¢ spread. Takers here are informed. Across these markets, fill losses ran about $7,500 a day against $11,400 a day in reward pools. The program covers the cost of being picked off about 1.5 times over, a margin consistent with roughly three effective competitors in the contest math above. But in 5 of the 51 markets, a single repricing cost more than the entire pool. The reward is fair rather than free. The edge is in avoiding the markets where news is coming.

The short programs look better. Repeating the measurement on programs lasting an hour or less, which carry 60% of the money, the picture changes by series. In hourly Miami temperature, Rotten Tomatoes and Trump-approval contracts, makers lost only 0.4–0.8¢ per contract filled, and the reward pools were 6–10 times their losses. In 15-minute currency contracts, makers seemed to make 1–3¢ per contract on their fills that day. But over a full week, marked to the final outcome, that vanished: maker results were +0.1¢, +1.0¢ and −0.4¢ per contract across three currency pairs, none significant. So fills roughly break even in these short markets, and the reward itself is the edge. The exception was 15-minute crypto, where traders watching the live Bitcoin price picked off stale quotes for 3.8¢ a contract, about equal to the pool. Adverse selection is high where fast public information exists and low where takers have no edge. That tells a liquidity provider where to quote. One more trap: I tried pricing the 15-minute currency contracts from a public minute-by-minute exchange-rate feed, and the model forecast far worse than the market itself. Near expiry, one pip is worth several cents of probability, so you need the exact settlement feed, not a close substitute.

Queues get cancelled, not consumed. I grouped a week of 15-minute fills into "visits", runs of trades at one price. In the currency markets a typical visit traded only about 10 contracts, while the reward books hold 1,000 or more at the best price. Behind a queue of 500, you'd fill on fewer than 0.5% of visits. Yet 40% of visits were followed by trades at the next worse price. The only way that happens is that the resting providers pull their orders when the exchange rate moves. So the risk in liquidity programs isn't your place in line. It's being the one who didn't cancel in time, which makes it a speed race: cancel as fast as everyone else and you collect the reward with almost no fills.

Which reward series pay best? Over one week I added up eight series' reward pools and set them against the makers' fill results, marked to final outcomes. Pools came to $256,000, fills netted to roughly zero (+$47,000), and every series came out ahead. How generous the rewards are varies about sixfold. Hourly Miami temperature paid 5.8¢ of reward per filled contract while its fills roughly broke even. The 15-minute currency contracts paid only about 1¢, because huge volume dilutes the pool. Florida gas-price markets were the trap: fills lost 3.3¢ a contract, eating 92% of the reward. For a newcomer, the ranking is hourly temperature first, then Trump approval, then currencies and crypto, and gas last.

Who wins in the hourly temperature markets? Over a week, 2.85 million contracts were filled in Miami hourly temperature, and the resting side made +1.2¢ a contract when marked to settlement, rising to about +3¢ in the final 15 minutes. Takers who traded in the direction the latest thermometer reading suggested did worst of all, losing 4.7¢ a contract. The public reading is already in the price. I also built a temperature-diffusion model from the weather service's minute data. It forecast far worse than the market (Brier 0.22 vs 0.08), because the contract settles on a different, more precise feed, and near the hour a few tenths of a degree decide the outcome. This is the most attractive reward series found: providers earn on both the rewards and the fills.

The same holds across Kalshi's daily high-temperature markets. Over a week, makers earned on fills in five of six cities, +0.81¢ a contract pooled (z = 2.2) across 7.5 million contracts. Rain markets showed no edge. Weather takers look like betting flow, not informed traders, which fits the favorite-longshot pattern found in weather prices earlier.

Arbitrage: when outcomes don't sum to $1

In a multi-outcome Polymarket event, exactly one outcome pays $1. Polymarket's neg-risk adapter lets a NO share in one outcome convert to a YES in every other outcome. So a full set of n NOs can be turned into n − 1 dollars right away, and the arithmetic of a Dutch book becomes:

buy every YES: cost Σ asks, receive $1 at resolution → only worth it if Σ asks < e^(−r·T) buy every NO: cost n − Σ bids, convert to n − 1 now → instant profit Σ bids − 1 (before fees)

I scanned all 2,100 active Polymarket events on October 14, 2026 using live best bid and ask quotes. Across the 632 multi-outcome events quoted on every outcome, prices summed to a median of 0.995.

  • Sell-side gaps (Σ bids > 1): 15 events, median 0.8¢, max 1.5¢, all with fees enabled. For example, the 2027 NBA champion summed to 1.010 across 30 separate books. That's fee-sized, with small quote sizes, and requires hitting dozens of books at once.
  • Buy-side gaps (Σ asks < 1): 116 events, but mostly not arbitrage. Some have incomplete outcome sets. In others the gap is smaller than T-bill interest. The 2028 party market summed to 0.990, which earns 0.5% a year when T-bills pay 4.25%. Buying every outcome only beats cash if the asks sum below e^(−0.0425 × 2.07) ≈ 0.916.

Arbitrage does exist. Saguillo et al. (2025) estimate about $40 million of realized arbitrage profit on Polymarket, using on-chain data and LLMs to find logically linked markets. But bots compete it away: what's left at the top of the book is about a cent and roughly the size of fees. Time value is a structural force here: long-dated contracts have to trade below their "fair" probability, or holding them loses to T-bills. That's one plausible contributor to the YES underpricing found above.

Can a simple options model beat Kalshi's daily Bitcoin markets?

I backtested Kalshi's "BTC above K at 5pm ET" contracts over 68 days (August–October 2026). Strikes were picked within ±6% of the price a day earlier, so the outcome couldn't influence the selection. I compared the market against a lognormal model using Deribit's DVOL implied volatility. Every statistic is clustered by day, since all strikes on a day share one Bitcoin path.

  • The market wins. 24 hours out, the market's Brier score was 0.099 against the model's 0.105 (t = 3.7), and the model gets negative weight when both are combined. Trading whenever the model disagreed with the quote by more than 3¢ plus the fee lost 7¢ a contract (t = −2.5). Six hours out, it broke even.
  • The look-ahead trap. My first run showed +9¢ a contract (t = 3.6) at six hours. Deribit stamps hourly bars at their start, so the model was seeing an hour of future prices. Re-stamped by close time, the edge was zero. Check whether a data source labels bars by open or by close.
  • A hint for later. Six hours out, prices were under-confident: 5–15¢ contracts won only 4% of the time. That's a possible short-horizon favorite-longshot effect, but 68 days is too few to trade on.
  • Out-of-sample follow-up. I fitted a single "stretch factor" on the first 34 days, pushing probabilities further from 50% (a logit slope of 1.21), and tested it on the next 34. It beat the raw market in a formal forecast comparison (Diebold–Mariano t = −4.0). Trading it after spread and fees earned about +3¢ a contract (t = 3.0), with buys and sells balanced. A version that also learned a direction had simply picked up Bitcoin's rally, and it faded. This is the most promising candidate of the course. It's still one regime and a short sample, and the form was chosen after a first look at the data, so it's pre-registered for forward paper trading rather than traded. (A bug along the way: sorting dates as text puts AUG before OCT before SEP.)
  • A likely reason. Bitcoin options have consistently overpriced future volatility. Deribit's implied-volatility index exceeded the volatility that followed by 9.5 points on average since 2021 (t = 5.6), though the gap has shrunk from 19 points in 2021 to 3 in 2026. These binary markets price with the same implied volatility, so their probability distributions are too wide and their prices sit too close to 50%. Pushing them away from 50% is effectively selling that volatility premium. It carries the same crash risk: in the worst month, realized volatility beat implied by 24 points.

Logical consistency across linked markets

Separate markets on related questions must obey probability logic, but no single order book enforces it. I checked the four live 2028 election markets on Polymarket (winner, Democratic nominee, Republican nominee, winning party) on October 14, 2026:

subset: X wins presidency ⊂ X wins nomination → P(win) ≤ P(nominee) executable if winner bid > nominee ask (sell win YES, buy nominee YES) additivity: Σ_{c in party} P(c wins) ≤ P(party wins) worth doing: gap g on capital K locked for T years → need g/K > e^(rT) − 1
  • Subset rule. Of 52 matched candidates, exactly one was violated in executable prices. Dwayne Johnson's presidency bid was 0.4¢ above his nomination ask, implying P(win | nominated) = 1.5. That's a tiny longshot book; every other name sat at the 0.1¢ tick floor.
  • An incomplete list looks underpriced. The winner market's mids add up to only 0.919. Buying every YES for 0.947 isn't arbitrage, because about 8% of the probability belongs to people who aren't listed. The same missing mass explains why Democratic candidates sum to 0.53 against 0.635 for the party.
  • Republican names were overpriced relative to the party. Bids on 24 candidates summed to 0.371, against a 0.350 ask for "Republican wins," a 2.1¢ gap. It isn't riskless, because a Republican could win as an independent. Most legs are at the tick floor, and the trade ties up about $23.6 for two years per $1 of payoff, which is roughly 0.09% total. Not worth it.

Lesson: logical arbitrage needs an exact implication, executable prices (not mids), real size and a short lockup. Check all four. Bots harvest the gaps that pass, so what's left is either in longshot tails or locked up for years (Saguillo et al. 2025).

  • The contract only prices the decision. Bonds actually rallied in the first minute, because an 87%-expected hike is only a 3bp surprise. Over the next hour, through the press conference, the 10-year yield rose 6bp and SPY fell 0.84%. The guidance carried most of the information, and the prediction market can't express it.

The realistic edge isn't reacting to the announcement. It's modeling how data surprises (CPI, payrolls) move the decision probabilities before the meeting.

Polymarket vs the bond market on the next Fed meeting

Fed funds futures settle at 100 minus the month's average overnight rate, so they imply the expected rate change at a meeting. The October 28 meeting changes the rate for all of November:

E[Δ] = (100 − Nov futures price) − today's EFFR P(+25bp) ≈ E[Δ] / 25bp

On October 14, 2026, the November contract (96.045) and EFFR (3.88%, from FRED) implied a +7.5bp expected move, or a 30% chance of a hike. Polymarket's five outcome buckets implied +7.2bp, a 28.5% chance. They agree within about 1¢, less than Polymarket's spread. Together with the 6-second reaction to the September decision and the Bitcoin contracts matching the options market, that's three checks showing liquid prediction markets track professional markets closely. Any edge has to be in questions no futures market prices.

Price-threshold markets are digital options

"Bitcoin above $82,000 on Friday at noon" is a cash-or-nothing digital option, and options math prices it. A digital is the slope of the call price with respect to strike, so a tight call spread replicates it:

digital D(K) = −∂C/∂K = e^(−rT)·N(d2), d2 = [ln(F/K) − σ²T/2] / (σ√T) (flat vol) with a smile σ(K): D = e^(−rT)·[ N(d2) − F·φ(d1)·√T · ∂σ/∂K ] (skew term) digital delta ≈ φ(d2) / (S·σ·√T) → at the money, 2 days, 40% vol: ≈ 4¢ per $100 move

I built the Deribit BTC option surface (449 options, 11 expiries), interpolated it to Polymarket's noon-ET and Kalshi's 5pm-ET settlement times, and compared it with every live threshold contract on October 14, 2026:

  • The venues track the options market. Mids were within 1.7¢ (Polymarket) and 1.6¢ (Kalshi) RMSE of the skew-consistent digital, against 3.3¢ for plain flat-vol N(d2). The crowd prices the skew.
  • The leftover gap is the carry. Near the money the venues sit 2–3¢ below options. One shift of the forward per expiry removes most of it (RMSE 0.4–1.2¢, about one spread). The implied center is $80–170 below Deribit's forward, which roughly matches the futures premium. Prediction-market traders center on spot, not the forward, and ignore the funding premium.
  • Small details are worth cents. Polymarket settles on Binance BTC/USDT (USDT = $0.9987, which shifts strikes by about $105), Kalshi on a 60-second CF index average, and Deribit at 08:00 UTC. With digital delta at 4¢ per $100, reconcile all of it before calling anything "mispriced."

Trading the gap means hedging with Deribit call spreads that settle at a different time and on a different index, with fees on both venues, while holding the carry premium. That's relative value with basis risk, not arbitrage.

The whole curve. I fitted a lognormal distribution to each expiry on both venues: Kalshi's two days and Polymarket's six, eight curves in all. Total variance rose steadily from one expiry to the next, so there was no calendar arbitrage. Implied volatility matched the options market within 0–3 points, including the dip for Saturday's quieter trading. The one systematic difference held at every maturity. The prediction markets centered on today's price (about $81.5k), while the options forward rose with the funding carry, from $81.6k to $81.75k over the week.

Which venue moves first? Minute by minute, Kalshi's quotes absorbed 63% of each Bitcoin move within the same minute and 80% within one minute. The size of the response, 0.19¢ per 0.01% move, matches the contract's theoretical delta of 0.22¢. Polymarket's matching contracts traded only about $3,600 in six hours, and their hourly contracts about $15 per strike, so their trade prices barely tracked Bitcoin at all. For Bitcoin binaries, Kalshi is where prices are discovered. Check a market's volume before building a strategy on it.

Sizing binary bets: Kelly for prediction markets

buy YES at price p with your probability q: f* = (q − p) / (1 − p) (NO side: f* = (p − q) / p) growth per bet G(f) = q·ln(1 + f(1−p)/p) + (1−q)·ln(1 − f)
  • Examples. A 5-point edge on a 50¢ contract means staking 10%. A 10-point edge means 20%. Near-certainties are dangerous: believing 97% against a 95¢ price says stake 40% for tiny growth.
  • Fees. With a 5-point edge on a 50¢ contract, a fee of 2% of the payout cuts long-run growth by 36%.
  • Time value. The same 5¢ edge is worth 26% a year if the market resolves next week, 2% if it takes three months, and 0.25% over two years, when T-bills pay 4.25%. Prediction-market edges only matter on short-dated contracts.
  • Your probability is an estimate. I simulated a trader with a real 3-point edge whose beliefs are off by ±5 points, over 2,000 bets. Full Kelly on those noisy beliefs lost money 72% of the time and lost 90%+ more than half the time. Half Kelly lost money 26% of the time, quarter Kelly 10%. Noisy beliefs turn "full Kelly" into over-betting.

Is a price really a probability?

Only under assumptions. Suppose traders hold beliefs qi, have wealth wi and constant relative risk aversion γ. Each trader's optimal YES position at price p, and the price that clears the market, are:

x = w(k − 1)/(1 − p + kp), k = [q(1−p) / ((1−q)p)]^(1/γ) log utility (γ = 1): x = w(q − p)/(p(1−p)) ⇒ p = Σ wᵢqᵢ / Σ wᵢ (wealth-weighted mean belief) risk-neutral (Manski): p = share of traders with q > p ⇒ mean belief anywhere in (p², 2p − p²)

I solved the equilibrium for 20,000 simulated traders:

  • Log utility makes the price exactly the average belief. Otherwise the gap grows with how much traders disagree, and it's zero at 50%. With a 5% average belief and a belief spread of 10 points, the price was +6.8¢ above the average when traders were barely risk-averse (γ = 0.1), and −4.0¢ below when they were very risk-averse (γ = 5).
  • Who trades sets the sign of the bias. Aggressive traders (γ < 1) overprice longshots, the classic favorite-longshot bias Kalshi's weather markets showed. Cautious traders (γ > 1) underprice them, the reverse pattern Polymarket showed in 2025–26. That's a consistent story, not proof.
  • The price is a money-weighted average. When wealth correlated 0.43 with belief, the average belief was 0.28 but the price was 0.48. A few large accounts can move the "probability."

One correction or many? Pooling noisy estimates

Kalshi's weather calibration slope varied by city, from 1.04 in Miami to 1.47 in New York. Is that real? A hierarchical model treats each city's true slope as drawn from a common distribution and asks how much of the spread is genuine:

b̂_g ~ N(b_g, s_g²), b_g ~ N(μ, τ²) → b̃_g = μ̂ + τ²/(τ² + s_g²) · (b̂_g − μ̂)

The estimated spread between cities was τ̂ = 0 (Cochran's Q 4.7 on 5 degrees of freedom), so the differences are noise. Every city's best estimate is the pooled 1.17. Estimating on the first half of the days and predicting the second half, city-by-city slopes did no better than the pooled one. Test for real differences before fitting a separate correction for every group.

Removing the "vig": Shin's insider model

A complete set of outcomes usually costs more than $1. Kalshi weather buckets summed to 1.045 at mid and 1.09 at the ask. To get probabilities you have to take that excess out, and the method matters. Shin's model says the market maker shades prices against a fraction z of insiders. Longshots carry more insider risk, so they get marked up more:

pᵢ = [ √(z² + 4(1−z)·πᵢ²/Σπ) − z ] / (2(1−z)), z chosen so Σp = 1 vs multiplicative pᵢ = πᵢ/Σπ · additive pᵢ = πᵢ − (Σπ−1)/n · power pᵢ = πᵢ^k

On 199 complete Kalshi weather days, simple division was the worst method. Buckets priced under 10¢ won 14 times; division predicted 18.9, Shin 16.9 and additive 15.5. Shin beat division on log loss (t = −3.4), with an implied insider share z ≈ 1–2%. Simple additive shading did best of all. So the markup sits mostly in the longshots, the same favorite-longshot pattern the calibration curve showed.

Does order flow predict prices?

I took the taker trades from the eight most active Polymarket markets and regressed each minute's price change on net signed buying (YES purchases minus YES sales, in dollars):

  • Impact is real. Kyle's λ was about 0.03¢ per $1,000 for a typical market. $10k of net buying moves the October Fed-hike contract about 1.1¢ but an NFL game only 0.05¢; sports books are far deeper.
  • Prediction is a mirage. Polymarket's own minute-level price history is stamped about a minute late. Taken at face value, it says order flow "predicts" the next minute's move in all eight markets, with t-statistics up to 40. Align it with actual trade prices and the effect shrinks to well under the 1¢ spread. Flow explains the same minute, not the next, just as Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov found for stocks.

Lesson: before believing any lead-lag result, check the data's timestamps against an independent source. A one-minute lag can manufacture a spectacular signal.

How volatile should a probability be?

Model the outcome as "a random walk X ends above zero." The fair price is then p = N(X/√τ), with τ the time left, and Itô's lemma gives the price's own dynamics:

dp = φ(N⁻¹(p)) / √τ · dW → Var(daily change) ≈ φ(N⁻¹(p))² · Δt/τ (no drift term: the price is a martingale; volatility peaks at 50% and grows as expiry nears)

Across 103,935 daily changes from 543 Polymarket events, the price shape matched exactly: the fitted exponent was 0.99 ± 0.05 against 1 in theory. Daily standard deviation ran 1.7¢ near 5%, 5.9¢ near 50% and 3.6¢ near 85%, all within a few tenths of a cent of theory. Time to expiry mattered only half as much as theory says (τ−0.53), because real news arrives in lumps rather than as a smooth signal. The exception was contracts at 90–98¢, which moved twice as much as predicted. Near-certainties suffer surprise jumps, which is why buying them lost money above. For a market maker, this formula sets how wide to quote and how much inventory to risk.

How fat is that tail? I counted one-day drops across 44,884 contract-days priced at 90¢ or more. Drops of 20¢ happened 39 times more often than the diffusion law predicts, and 40¢ drops about 2,800 times more often. The kurtosis of standardized changes was about 1,000. Contracts priced 90–95¢ eventually lost 9.1% of the time, against the 7.2% their price implied. Near-certainties don't drift; they sit still and then jump. Don't size them with volatility or normal-distribution risk numbers.

Do prices move too much? A one-line rationality test

A rational forecast is a martingale: its expected next value equals its current value. That alone pins down how much it should move. Add up the squared daily changes all the way to the 0-or-1 outcome. On average that sum must equal the uncertainty the forecast started with (Augenblick & Rabin 2021):

E[ Σ (π_{t+1} − π_t)² ] = π_0 (1 − π_0) (any sampling frequency; no model needed) proof per step: E[(Δπ)²] = E[π²_{t+1}] − E[π²_t] = E[u_t − u_{t+1}], u = π(1−π)

Across 1,625 resolved Polymarket markets, the ratio was 1.31 ± 0.06, and 1.38 after skipping the arbitrary opening days. A simulated rational learner with the same quote noise gave 1.02. Polymarket prices swing 30–40% more than the information justifies. The excess is largest for longshots (1.48), the same fact as the cheap YES contracts winning more often than priced. Daily changes reverse slightly (autocorrelation −0.08), but that's small next to a 1¢ spread. Apply the same test to your own forecasts: a ratio far above 1 means you're jumpy, far below 1 means you're sluggish.

"Free money" at 95¢? When to hold, when to sell

Holding a YES contract to resolution is worth your probability times a discount for the time your money is tied up. Selling it now is worth the bid:

sell now iff p − s > q·e^(−rτ) (s = half-spread, τ = time left, r = T-bill rate) agree with the market (q = p): sell if τ > −ln(1 − s/p)/r → 95¢ contract, 1¢ spread: 91 days

A popular retail strategy is to buy contracts once they reach 95¢ and "collect 5% safely." I tested it on 1,625 resolved Polymarket markets: buy the first day either side reaches the threshold, pay half a cent over, and hold to resolution:

Trading the overreaction

If prices move too much, betting against big moves should pay. Across 1,625 resolved markets, after a daily jump of 20¢ or more, prices gave back about 5¢ over the next five days (t = 4). The bigger the jump, the bigger the reversal. After paying a 1¢ half-spread in and out, though, only the largest moves still earned anything (+3¢, t = 2.5). That isn't significant once you account for testing nine variations, and right after a big jump is exactly when order books are thinnest. A strong statistical signal can still be a weak trade.

Kelly when exactly one outcome wins

For temperature buckets or election candidates, sizing each bucket as its own binary bet is wrong. Kelly's 1956 horse-race solution handles all the buckets at once:

rank outcomes by qᵢ/pᵢ; include i while qᵢ/pᵢ > R, R = (1 − Σ_S q)/(1 − Σ_S p) stake fraction fᵢ = qᵢ − R·pᵢ (keep R in cash)

Take six buckets priced (3, 12, 35, 33, 15, 6)¢ against your beliefs (2, 10, 30, 40, 15, 3)%. The solution stakes 11.4% on the 33¢ bucket and 2.0% on the 15¢ bucket, even though that one is priced exactly at your belief. It pays off when your main bet loses, so it's a hedge. Long-run growth is 10% higher than sizing each bucket separately. The closed form matched a numerical optimizer exactly. As before, belief errors are deadly: with 25% noise in your probabilities, full Kelly loses money on average, while half Kelly still grows.

Can the weather service's own forecast beat the weather market?

The obvious information source for "high temperature in Chicago tomorrow" is the National Weather Service's model output (GFS MOS). I fitted a bias-corrected normal distribution around the previous day's forecast for each city, then tested it on 116 later city-days. Measured by log loss, where lower is better, the market won easily: 1.20 against 1.81 for the model. Blending in any share of the model made the forecast worse, and trading on disagreements lost 1–2¢ a contract. By the time the market price is taken at midnight, traders already have newer model runs and live observations. Public, stale information is already in the price. An edge needs newer or better-processed information.

Are the top Polymarket traders skilled, or lucky?

I matched 1.07 million taker trades ($781M) by 235,867 wallets in 319 resolved 2026 markets against the outcomes. The key is the right null hypothesis. Everyone in a market faces the same outcome, so I simulated each market's result once, for all wallets together, using its average traded price as the probability, 500 times over. Then I asked how often each wallet's simulated profit beat its real one.

  • No wallet's profit is distinguishable from luck. Not one of 38,193 active wallets passed a 5% false-discovery-rate test. The top ten earners, from $0.17M to $5.1M, had p-values between 0.06 and 0.84; the $5.1M wallet made it on just seven markets. Big bets on a few outcomes produce big numbers by chance.
  • Some skill shows up in returns. Wallets in the top fifth by return in the first half of 2026 earned a median +1.3% on their trading in the second half, against −0.5% for the bottom fifth (rank correlation +0.07, p = 0.001). Skill shows up as small, steady percentage returns, not as headline profits.

If you copy-trade, rank by return per dollar traded across many markets, never by total profit on a leaderboard.

Do election prices carry a risk premium?

If an election outcome moved the stock market, investors would pay extra for the contract that pays off in the bad scenario, and prices would drift away from probabilities. I regressed daily changes in Polymarket's 2024 "Trump wins" price on stock, bond and currency returns. The S&P 500 beta was essentially zero (correlation +0.02), which makes the implied risk premium about 0.1¢. The signs on the famous "Trump trades" came out right: solar stocks down, the dollar up against the peso, bitcoin up. But each was only marginally significant. One trap appeared again: Polymarket's daily timestamp falls on the previous evening in New York. Shifting by one day produced a spurious +0.10 correlation with the S&P and flipped the other signs.

Recession contracts are different. Polymarket's "US recession in 2025?" fell 0.96¢ for every 1% rise in the S&P (t = −4.9), with a correlation of −0.83 on days the market moved more than 2%. A contract that pays when everything else falls is insurance, and insurance costs more than its expected payout:

price − true probability ≈ −(price change per 100% market move) × equity premium × years recession 2025: 0.96 × 5% × 1.1 ≈ 5¢ (on a contract that averaged 21¢)

So recession, crash and disaster contracts are priced above their true probability, by an estimated 3–5¢ here. Elections and sports aren't. Selling such contracts is selling insurance: you earn a premium, but you lose exactly when the rest of your portfolio does.

What the research says

  • Markets beat polls. Iowa Electronic Markets prices were closer to the final result than polls in 74% of 964 comparisons, 1988–2004, and significantly better more than 100 days out (Berg, Nelson & Rietz 2008).
  • Calibration decays with horizon. Prices are well calibrated near expiry but get pulled toward 50% for distant events, a favorite-longshot pattern that time value makes hard to exploit (Page & Clemen 2013).
  • Why longshots are overbet in betting markets: people misperceive small probabilities (prospect theory) more than they seek risk (Snowberg & Wolfers 2010).
  • Arbitrage is real and bot-dominated: about $40M extracted on Polymarket (Saguillo et al. 2025).

Put together with the data above: prediction-market prices are good forecasts, with biases that are small, largest in thin and long-dated markets, and mostly eaten by fees and time value. The quant edge is in short-dated markets, better models than the crowd, fast arbitrage and market making. That's the same lesson as everywhere else in this course.
